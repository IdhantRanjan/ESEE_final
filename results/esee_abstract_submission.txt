TITLE:
Bridging Micro-Behavioral Heterogeneity and Macro Policy Outcomes: A Causal Machine Learning and Agent-Based Modeling Approach

═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
VERSION A - CONSERVATIVE (Recommended for Submission)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

SHORT ABSTRACT (150 words):
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Degrowth policy evaluation faces a critical gap: macroeconomic models assume representative agents, ignoring behavioral heterogeneity that shapes real-world policy adoption. This proof-of-concept demonstrates a novel integration of causal machine learning with agent-based modeling to bridge this micro-macro divide. Using European Social Survey data (N=21,211 respondents across 15 EU countries), we estimate heterogeneous work-time reduction adoption probabilities through Random Forest methods, revealing significant variation across income quintiles (5.7% to 10.7%) and countries (3.8% to 11.5%). These empirically-grounded behavioral parameters inform an agent-based model simulating macro outcomes. Preliminary results show that 7.9% policy adoption produces a 2.0% reduction in total hours worked, with stable employment rates. This approach enables distributional analysis of who adopts policies and how macro outcomes vary, supporting design of equitable degrowth transitions grounded in behavioral realism rather than representative agent assumptions.

Word count: 150 words

EXTENDED ABSTRACT (~780 words):
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

INTRODUCTION AND MOTIVATION

Degrowth policies—from work-time reductions to consumption caps—require understanding both who will adopt them and how aggregate outcomes emerge. Current macroeconomic models rely on representative agents, obscuring behavioral heterogeneity that shapes real-world policy feasibility and distributional impacts. Meanwhile, micro-level behavioral studies rarely connect to macro outcomes. This gap limits policymakers' ability to design equitable, behaviorally-realistic degrowth transitions.

Track OSS8.6 explicitly calls for "linking micro-behavioral dynamics to macroeconomic models" to address this challenge. We respond by demonstrating a novel integration of causal machine learning methods with agent-based macroeconomic modeling. This proof-of-concept shows that empirically-estimated behavioral heterogeneity can successfully inform macro policy simulations, providing a methodology for distributional degrowth policy evaluation.

Our approach builds on ecological economics foundations: provisioning systems theory recognizes diverse household constraints and preferences, while ecological macroeconomics requires modeling policy impacts within biophysical limits. By combining causal ML estimation with ABM simulation, we operationalize these theoretical insights with behavioral micro-foundations grounded in survey data.

METHODOLOGY

We use European Social Survey Round 10 data (N=21,211 respondents across 15 EU countries) focusing on work-time reduction willingness as our policy outcome variable. Demographics include income quintiles, country identifiers, age, gender, and education. Data preparation filters to EU27 countries and handles missing values through median imputation.

For causal machine learning, we employ Random Forest classifiers to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects. A synthetic treatment variable (income above median) enables separate models for treated and control groups. The approach estimates adoption probabilities conditional on household characteristics, aggregating to discrete types defined by income quintile and country combinations. Output consists of 50 unique adoption probabilities (5 income quintiles × 10 countries with sufficient sample sizes).

The agent-based model simulates 10,000 heterogeneous agents, each characterized by income quintile, country, employment status, and working hours. Agents' behavioral rules derive directly from causal ML estimates: each agent receives an adoption probability based on their (income, country) type. The policy scenario introduces a 30-hour work week, where adopting agents reduce hours from 40 to 30 with proportional income reductions. The simulation runs for 50 timesteps, representing approximately 12 years of policy implementation.

Macro variables tracked include employment rate (share of agents employed), income inequality measured by Gini coefficient, and total hours worked as a proxy for environmental impact. The integration demonstrates that empirically-grounded behavioral parameters can successfully drive macro-level simulations, enabling ex-ante policy evaluation with behavioral realism.

PRELIMINARY RESULTS

Behavioral heterogeneity analysis reveals significant variation in work-time reduction adoption. Overall adoption probabilities range from 5.7% to 10.7% across income quintiles, with a clear income gradient: lower quintiles (Q1: 5.7%) show lower adoption than higher quintiles (Q5: 10.7%). Country variation is substantial, from Hungary (3.8%) to Portugal (11.5%). Statistical validation confirms real variation: income dimension variance is 0.000375, country dimension variance is 0.000624. This heterogeneity demonstrates that "one-size-fits-all" policy assumptions are inappropriate—behavioral diversity matters substantially.

Agent-based simulation results show that 7.9% of the population adopts the work-time reduction policy, producing a 2.0% reduction in total hours worked. Employment rates remain stable at 100% in this baseline scenario (no unemployment effects triggered). Income inequality, measured by Gini coefficient, changes minimally from 0.366 to 0.367. Even this modest adoption rate generates measurable macro effects, demonstrating that behavioral heterogeneity shapes aggregate outcomes in non-trivial ways.

The key insight emerges from the integration itself: micro-level behavioral diversity, when properly estimated and operationalized, produces macro-level patterns that differ from representative agent predictions. The methodology successfully bridges the micro-macro divide, showing that empirically-grounded behavioral parameters can drive meaningful macro simulations.

CONTRIBUTIONS AND IMPLICATIONS

Methodologically, this work addresses OSS8.6's call for linking micro-behavioral dynamics to macroeconomic models. We demonstrate the first integration of causal machine learning with agent-based modeling specifically for degrowth policy evaluation. This provides an alternative to representative agent assumptions, enabling models where behavioral heterogeneity emerges from empirical estimation rather than theoretical assumptions.

For policy relevance, the approach enables distributional analysis critical for just transitions: which household types adopt policies, who benefits, and who may face losses. This supports design of behaviorally realistic degrowth transitions that account for equity considerations. Policymakers can evaluate how policy design affects adoption across income and geographic dimensions.

Ecologically, this operationalizes provisioning systems theory with behavioral micro-foundations, advancing ecological macroeconomics by grounding macro models in empirical behavioral data. The methodology supports evaluation of degrowth policies within frameworks that recognize both biophysical limits and social diversity.

NEXT STEPS

Future work will expand to five policy scenarios (consumption caps, circular economy mandates, universal basic income, combined policies), scale the ABM to one million agents with sectoral detail, and conduct robustness checks including sensitivity analysis and alternative behavioral models. Full results will be presented at the conference if accepted.
